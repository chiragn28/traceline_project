# Traceline — Business Case

## The Problem

Picture a mid-sized fintech in Dubai during its annual SOC 2 or ISO 27001 audit. The external auditor asks a simple-sounding question: "Show me everyone who accessed customer PII in the last quarter, and confirm each access was authorized." The compliance officer doesn't have a system that answers this — they have a patchwork of database logs, a spreadsheet someone updates when they remember to, and a Slack thread of "who touched the customers table last week?" The answer takes days to assemble, is incomplete, and nobody fully trusts it. This isn't an edge case; it's the default state at firms that never built dedicated data-lineage tooling, because access logging was always treated as an infrastructure afterthought, not a compliance asset.

The pain shows up on a rolling basis, not just once a year. Every quarter, whoever owns the access review — usually a compliance lead, sometimes the CTO's team when compliance doesn't have the technical access to pull the data themselves — burns days manually reconstructing who touched which tables, cross-referencing raw query logs against an org chart that's already out of date. When something actually goes wrong — a customer complaint about unexpected access, or a departing employee whose access needs retroactive review — the same gap turns from an annoyance into real exposure: nobody can produce, quickly and confidently, a clean record of who accessed what data and why. The people caught in the middle are the compliance officer signing an attestation they can't fully verify, the CTO whose engineers get pulled off product work to run one-off SQL queries for auditors, the DPO answering for data residency and sensitive-field access without a system built to answer that question directly, and the auditor themselves, who has no choice but to accept manual, self-reported evidence.

## Why Now

That gap between what compliance officers are asked to prove and what their systems can actually show them isn't new — but three forces are converging right now to make it far more costly to leave unresolved. First, regulatory attention on data governance and access controls across UAE and GCC financial regulators has been visibly increasing in recent years, part of a broader push to bring the region's compliance expectations in line with international standards; the exact rule changes driving this still need to be confirmed with real sources rather than asserted here. Second, the number of fintech and trading firms licensed through hubs like DIFC and ADGM has grown substantially over the past several years — meaning there are simply more firms today who need this kind of tooling than existed five years ago, firms past the "we'll figure it out later" stage of company-building but well before the size where they'd build this in-house. Third, and perhaps most immediately, the enterprise customers and banking partners these fintechs sell to are increasingly making SOC 2 or ISO 27001 certification a condition of doing business at all — which means the audit-trail gap from Section 1 isn't just a regulator's problem anymore, it's now a sales-cycle blocker.

Together, these three forces mean the cost of not having this system has shifted from an annual audit headache to a recurring risk to revenue, licensing, and enterprise deals — which sets up exactly why the cost of inaction is now large enough for someone to pay to fix it.

## Cost of Inaction

If the audit-trail gap is now a sales-cycle blocker rather than just a compliance headache, the natural next question is what it actually costs a firm to leave it unfixed. The costs stack up on four fronts. There's the direct one everyone worries about first — regulatory fines and penalties for inadequate data governance or access controls — though the actual amounts a firm might face need to be confirmed with real regulatory sources rather than assumed here. There's the audit itself: a failed or delayed SOC 2 or ISO 27001 certification doesn't just cost the fee for re-running the audit, it costs the credibility of every partner and customer conversation that was waiting on that certification to close. There's the quieter, recurring cost of engineering time — every quarter that compliance can't self-serve an access review, it's an engineer, not a compliance analyst, hand-writing SQL to answer an auditor's question, which is time that isn't going into the product the company is actually trying to sell. And there's the sharpest cost of all: a deal with an enterprise customer or banking partner that stalls or dies outright because the fintech couldn't produce clean, credible access evidence on the timeline the deal required.

None of these four have hard numbers behind them yet in this draft — they're the real, plausible mechanisms by which the problem costs money, but the actual figures (fine amounts, re-audit fees, engineer-hours per quarter, size of a specific stalled deal) need to be confirmed as real data, not asserted. What's clear even without the numbers is that these costs compound the longer a firm waits, which is exactly the gap Traceline is built to close.

## The Solution

That's the gap Traceline closes: instead of the manual scramble described in Sections 1–3, it gives compliance and engineering teams a single, always-on system of record for who accessed what data, when, and whether that access was allowed. In plain terms, Traceline connects read-only to a company's Postgres database, quietly tails its access logs, and builds a live map of which tables exist, which pipelines and people actually touch them, and how often. Compliance teams tag sensitive tables (customer PII, financial records, and so on) and set simple rules about where that data is allowed to be accessed from; Traceline then automatically flags any access that breaks those rules, instead of someone finding out during an audit, months later. When it's time for a quarterly review or an external audit, instead of hand-assembling spreadsheets, someone generates a hash-stamped, timestamped CSV report straight from the system — evidence that's tamper-evident and takes minutes, not days, to produce.

The current demo shows exactly this end-to-end: connecting a Postgres data source, visualizing the resulting access lineage graph (which pipelines and people touch which tables), tagging tables by sensitivity, setting region rules, seeing violations surface automatically, running a full access-review cycle with sign-off, and exporting the audit report — all live at traceline-pi.vercel.app, not a mockup. Today that connector layer is Postgres-only and region-matching is a manually configured CIDR map rather than fully automated geolocation, which is an honest reflection of where an MVP should draw its scope. The natural next steps — more database connectors, more automated region detection, finer-grained (column-level, not just table-level) lineage — are a roadmap, not a claim about what's built today.

Traceline is also deliberately built to sit alongside the tools these firms already run, not replace them. It's complementary to the data-movement and transformation layer (Fivetran, Airbyte, dbt) that builds a firm's warehouse in the first place, and to the BI/analytics layer (Metabase, Looker, Power BI) whose service accounts generate a meaningful share of the access events Traceline is watching for. Most importantly, it's complementary to the SOC 2/ISO 27001 compliance-automation platforms firms already use to manage their audit evidence — Vanta, Drata, and Secureframe are the category leaders here, each already supporting hundreds of integrations to auto-collect control evidence.[^1] Traceline's hash-stamped access reports are built to be exactly the kind of artifact those platforms want to ingest as evidence for their access-control checks, not a competing GRC workflow tool — it's the piece none of them do well: table-level, database-native access lineage.

## Why Me / Why This Team

That solution isn't a theoretical read of the problem — it's a rebuild of something already proven to work in exactly this environment. Before Traceline, I worked as an AI Engineer on the Data Engineering team at Deriv, a Dubai-based online trading company, maintaining production data pipelines on GCP and building the kind of internal tooling most fintechs only realize they need after an audit forces the question. One of those tools was an internal data usage tracker that mapped consumption of Bronze, Silver, and Gold medallion-layer tables across different service accounts — giving the data governance team real visibility into who and what was actually touching production data, and cutting the time spent on ad-hoc usage audits. That's functionally a smaller-scale version of the exact problem Traceline solves for MENA fintech and trading firms broadly: know what's touching your data, and make that visible instead of reconstructed after the fact.

Traceline isn't a bet on an outside read of what compliance teams need — it's a generalized, productized version of a tool already built and used inside a real trading firm operating in this exact region and regulatory environment. That's the difference between guessing what MENA fintechs need and having already built a working version of it from the inside.

## Defensibility

Having actually built this problem's smaller cousin inside a regional trading firm is also the root of why this is defensible, not just credible. The global competitive landscape splits into three tiers, none of which is actually positioned to win this fight. At the enterprise end sit broad data-governance catalogs — Collibra, Informatica IDMC, IBM Knowledge Catalog — built for large organizations with dedicated governance programs and priced/implemented accordingly.[^2] Below them, modern cloud-native catalogs like Atlan, Alation, and Ataccama serve data teams already standardized on Snowflake and dbt.[^2] The closest real analog to Traceline is the data-access-governance specialist tier — BigID, Immuta, and Satori, which explicitly builds audit trails for data access — plus Varonis, which just paid $25M in March 2025 to acquire Cyral, a database-activity-monitoring startup, specifically to "reinvent database activity monitoring."[^3] That acquisition is a useful signal two ways: it confirms this category is real and valuable enough for a public security company to buy into, and it confirms none of these players are MENA-focused — every one of them is a US/EU-headquartered vendor selling into US/EU enterprise budgets, where DIFC, UAE Central Bank, and broader GCC regulatory nuance is a rounding error on the roadmap, not a priority.

The regional layer doesn't close that gap either — it's solving different problems. The UAE has around 34 identified RegTech startups, only 6 of them funded,[^4] and the visible players are working adjacent niches: Amani AI (the first RegTech company licensed in DIFC) focuses on identity verification and KYC/AML; Azakaw offers a broader compliance platform spanning transaction surveillance, conduct-of-business compliance, and risk management; DX Compliance (Abu Dhabi) targets anti-financial-crime and AML; Moltazim (Saudi) aggregates regulatory rules into one platform.[^5] None of them do table-level, database-native access lineage — that gap, between a young regional regtech scene solving KYC/AML/transaction-surveillance problems and a mature global data-governance market that's ignoring this region, is exactly the wedge: a small, regionally-focused team can move faster and go deeper on this one niche than a company juggling a global product line ever will, and can build the kind of direct trust with regional compliance teams — speaking their regulatory language, being reachable, being local — that a distant global vendor selling a generic product can't replicate quickly, even if they eventually decided the market was worth chasing.

That regional focus is also what defines the actual size of the opportunity worth defending — which is the market this needs to be sized against next.

## Market Size

The regional focus argued for in Section 6 only works as a business if the first addressable slice of that region is actually large enough to build a company on. There are real, public anchor numbers here, even without a precise bottom-up count yet. DIFC's own Innovation Hub — the largest fintech accelerator/innovation community in the region — has issued over 700 innovation licenses to date.[^6] Separately, Tracxn's own regtech tracking counts 34 identified RegTech startups in the UAE, only 6 of them funded[^4] — a young, real, and clearly underserved category rather than a crowded one, which supports a wedge rather than undercuts it. Both DIFC and ADGM also run dedicated fintech regulatory sandboxes (DIFC's Innovation Testing License, ADGM's RegLab),[^7] which is itself a signal: firms coming through those programs are, by definition, newer, growing, and past the stage of building compliance tooling in-house, exactly the profile Traceline is priced and positioned for first.

## Business Model

That market-sizing gap doesn't block writing a business model — it just means the pricing has to be structured to work whether the first addressable market turns out to be forty firms or four hundred. Traceline is priced as a B2B SaaS compliance tool sold jointly to compliance and engineering leadership: compliance owns the need and the budget, engineering has to approve the read-only database access and implementation, so the sale (and the product itself) has to satisfy both. Pricing is structured as a small number of flat tiers rather than a-la-carte per-seat or per-source pricing, because a compliance buyer thinks in budget lines, not usage metering — something like a Starter tier for a firm with one or two data sources and quarterly reporting needs, a Growth tier for firms with multiple data sources, more frequent access reviews, and multiple compliance/engineering seats, and an Enterprise tier for firms that need custom region rules, dedicated support, and audit-grade SLAs.
